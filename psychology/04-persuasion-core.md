# The Persuasion Core

The mental model behind every tactic in this pack, plus the catalog of load-bearing principles.
Read this before you write a page, a price, or an ad — once you hold the three engines, you can
*derive* the right move instead of copying one. Read it again whenever a page underperforms and
you need to name what is actually missing.

---

## 0. Name the dial first

Almost every persuasion tactic is **direction-dependent**. It works at one end of an arousal
spectrum and backfires at the other. Decide the end before you decide the tactic.

| | **Trust pole** (calm / premium / considered) | **Urgency pole** (energetic / impulsive / playful) |
|---|---|---|
| Typical brand | dental clinic, B2B SaaS, financial tool, professional course, therapist | flash-sale marketplace, mobile game, festival ticket, snack DTC, youth apparel |
| Buying mode | slow, comparison-heavy, risk-dominant | fast, low-stakes, novelty-dominant |
| Color / motion | muted, cool, slow easing, generous space | saturated, warm, fast motion, dense grids |
| Persuasion emphasis | belief, mechanism, autonomy, proof | momentum, novelty, contrast, immediacy |
| Scarcity | honest status/cohort framing only | live inventory and real deadlines, still honest |
| The failure to fear | reads as pushy → distrust → bounce | reads as flat → ignored → no action |

**Working rule:** if a tactic below is labelled *Urgency pole*, and your brand sits at the trust
pole, do not soften it — **invert it** and spend the attention you saved on the one element that
must be seen. A calm page makes a single quiet accent enormously loud; that is the trust pole's
structural advantage, not a consolation prize.

Positioning determines the pole. Decide it in
[../brand/01-positioning-and-category.md](../brand/01-positioning-and-category.md) before you
touch this file's catalog.

## 1. The three engines

Every named effect further down is a special case of one of these three. Learn the engines and the
catalog becomes a lookup table rather than a memory test. This framing follows the school of
thought running through Nick Kolenda's consumer-psychology work, Cialdini's influence research, and
the fluency literature in cognitive psychology.

### Engine 1 — Processing fluency
**Claim.** The easier something is to perceive, read, and understand, the more people like it — and they misattribute that ease to the *object*, not to their own processing. Fluent things get judged as truer, better made, safer, and cheaper to act on.

**Mechanism.** The brain has no read-out of *why* something felt easy. Ease arrives as a vague positive signal and attaches to whatever is in front of you; friction attaches the same way, as doubt.

**Derive from it:**
- Legible type, adequate contrast, and short sentences raise *perceived truthfulness* independent of content → never render a trust claim in low contrast or a decorative face.
- A page that meets the expectation set by its own name outperforms a clever one. A page titled "Pricing" shows prices on load.
- One repeated rhythm (same section pattern, spacing scale, button) compounds fluency down the page; novelty per section reads as noise.
- Rhyme and rhythm raise believability of a line — which is also why fluency is *not* evidence of honesty. See §4.

**The trap.** Fluency makes false claims land as easily as true ones. That is why §4 is not optional.

### Engine 2 — Spreading activation (association)
**Claim.** Any cue — a word, color, shape, angle, font, stock photo, neighbouring logo — activates a cloud of related concepts, and that cloud lands on the brand whether you intended it or not.

**Mechanism.** Concepts are stored as a network. Activating one node partially activates its neighbours, and the residue biases the next judgement made in that context.

**Derive from it:**
- Round shapes → soft, safe, friendly; angular → precise, technical, competent. Pick per archetype ([../brand/02-identity-archetype-and-naming.md](../brand/02-identity-archetype-and-naming.md)).
- A "small" word beside a price ("just", "from", "a day") drags smallness onto the number; a threat word beside a promise drags threat onto the brand.
- A crowded layout activates *cheap*; empty space activates *expensive*. Nothing was said about price either way.
- Whatever sits nearest the CTA fuses into the imagined action — the reassurance line becomes part of the click, and so does the legal warning.

**The trap.** Association is indiscriminate; it cannot be aimed at only the flattering neighbours. Audit imagery, error colors, and adjacent logos for what they drag in.

### Engine 3 — Mental simulation
**Claim.** People decide by running a quick internal movie: **imagined outcome minus imagined effort**. Desire is the remainder — so you have two independent levers, not one.

**Mechanism.** Simulating an experience recruits some of the machinery of having it. What is easy to picture feels more likely, more available, and more already-yours.

**Derive from it:**
- Concrete beats abstract, always. "A written treatment plan in the same visit" simulates; "patient-centred care" does not.
- Naming the *first step* shrinks perceived effort more than shortening the process ("answer four questions" beats "quick and easy onboarding").
- Present tense and second person raise simulation depth; past tense and third person kill it.
- Every removed form field, pre-filled default, and unit you convert for the reader subtracts directly from the effort term.
- Product-in-use imagery simulates better than an isolated hero shot — except where the buyer must judge fine attributes, where isolation wins.

**The trap.** A vivid outcome with a vague process reads as a promise you cannot keep. Vivid outcome **and** concrete process.

### Deriving a tactic in 30 seconds

| Symptom on the page | Which engine is starved | Derived move |
|---|---|---|
| Visitors read but don't believe | fluency + simulation | add mechanism ("because…"), swap abstractions for specifics |
| Visitors believe but feel nothing | simulation | make the outcome scene concrete, use second person, present tense |
| Page feels "cheap" with no bad element | association | more whitespace, fewer saturated elements, fewer type sizes |
| Visitors stall at the button | simulation (effort term) | name the first step, pre-answer cost/commitment right under the CTA |
| Everything looks equally important | fluency + attention | one focal element per viewport; demote the rest |

## 2. The principle catalog

Read each block as: **What → Mechanism → Apply → Breaks when → Dial.** *Dial* tells you which pole
the tactic belongs to, and what the inverse looks like at the other pole.

### Family A — Making a true thing believable

#### Reason-why ("because")
- **What.** A claim carrying its cause outperforms the same claim bare.
- **Mechanism.** A stated reason satisfies the mind's demand for justification; it also raises fluency because the sentence resolves rather than dangling. Classic compliance research found even thin reasons lifted agreement for small requests — but thin reasons collapse when stakes rise.
- **Apply.** Rule: **no promise ships without its mechanism.** Not "your data is safe" → "your notes never leave your device, because the analysis runs locally and the server only ever sees a hash."
- **Breaks when.** The "because" is circular ("it's the best because it's the best") — this reads as evasion and costs more credibility than saying nothing.
- **Dial.** Both poles. At the trust pole it is the single highest-leverage copy move.

#### Authority and provenance
- **What.** Credentials, origin stories, and verifiable track record transfer credibility to claims.
- **Mechanism.** Under uncertainty people delegate judgement to a source they can check, which is cheaper than evaluating the claim itself.
- **Apply.** Prefer **provenance over titles**: who built it, why, what they did before, what it was first used for. One checkable specific ("built by the team that ran the clinic's own scheduling desk for six years") beats three unverifiable superlatives. Put credentials where the doubt is, not in a bio nobody reaches.
- **Breaks when.** Authority is borrowed rather than owned (logo walls of companies that were merely visitors), or stacked so heavily it reads as compensation.
- **Dial.** Trust pole leans hard on provenance and mechanism. Urgency pole leans on popularity and recency instead.

#### Social proof — and its three conditions
- **What.** Evidence that comparable people already chose this reduces the perceived risk of choosing it.
- **Mechanism.** Others' behaviour is treated as distributed evidence about an unknown. It is a *shortcut for uncertainty*, which is why its power scales with how uncertain the visitor is.
- **The three conditions.** Proof works to the degree it is:
  1. **Similar** — the proof-giver resembles the reader in role, stage, or situation. A solo dentist is unmoved by a hospital group's endorsement.
  2. **Uncertain** — the reader genuinely doesn't know how to judge. Proof adds little where the reader is already expert or the choice is trivial.
  3. **Specific** — a concrete situation, number, or before/after that could in principle be false.
- **Why vague proof backfires.** "Loved by thousands" is unfalsifiable, so it carries no information — and an unfalsifiable claim in a credibility slot *signals* that a falsifiable one was unavailable. Vague praise actively lowers trust relative to silence.
- **Apply.** Rules: use real names and roles; quote the *situation*, not the adjective; show the small drawback alongside the praise (a visible, minor imperfection reads more credible than uniform five-star praise); if you have no proof yet, say so plainly and let mechanism and provenance carry the page.
- **Breaks when.** It is invented, purchased, or aggregated so loosely it cannot be checked. This is a forbidden zone, not a grey one — see §4.
- **Dial.** Both poles; the *form* differs. Trust pole: named, detailed, few. Urgency pole: volume, recency, live counts.

#### Liking and unity
- **What.** People comply more with sources they like, and far more with sources they experience as *part of the same group as themselves*.
- **Mechanism.** Liking is a warmth signal that lowers perceived adversarial intent. Unity is stronger and different: shared identity blurs the self/other boundary, so the source's recommendation is processed closer to one's own conclusion.
- **Apply.** Unity beats flattery. Name the shared identity honestly ("we build the thing we needed as a two-person studio"); use "we/our" for the shared category, "you/your" for the reader's outcome. Describe genuine enjoyment of the craft where it is true — it reads as motivation and non-automation, and it dodges the reactance that a direct self-praising claim triggers.
- **Breaks when.** The claimed in-group is fake, or warmth substitutes for competence. Warmth without competence reads as nice-but-useless.
- **Dial.** Both. Trust pole favours quiet unity and competence-first ordering; urgency pole favours visible enthusiasm and personality.

### Family B — Momentum and commitment

#### Reciprocity
- **What.** An unrequested, useful gift creates a felt obligation to reciprocate.
- **Mechanism.** Reciprocity is a deep social-exchange norm; unmet obligation is uncomfortable enough that people repay disproportionately.
- **Apply.** Give something that is genuinely useful *without* the ask attached: a real calculator, a template, a diagnostic, an unpaywalled guide that solves the problem for the DIY reader. Make the gift specific to the visitor's situation (personalised beats generic) and unexpected.
- **Breaks when.** The gift is transparently a lead magnet with a toll gate, or the ask follows too fast. Then it reads as a transaction and the obligation never forms.
- **Dial.** Both poles. Trust pole: give the substantive thing, ask later, ask once.

#### Commitment and consistency
- **What.** A small initial action makes a larger congruent action more likely.
- **Mechanism.** People infer their own attitudes from their behaviour and then defend the inferred self-image. Public, effortful, and voluntary commitments bind hardest.
- **Apply.** Design a **ladder**, not a leap: read → try the interactive tool → save a result → create an account → pay. Each rung must be genuinely useful on its own so the ladder isn't a trap. Keep every rung reversible.
- **Breaks when.** The escalation is hidden (a "free scan" that turns out to be a sales call), or the rungs are effortful but useless. Both convert consistency into resentment.
- **Dial.** Both. Trust pole: fewer, larger, clearly-labelled rungs. Urgency pole: many micro-steps.

#### Endowed progress
- **What.** A task with some progress already granted gets finished more often than the same task started at zero.
- **Mechanism.** Progress toward a visible goal accelerates near the goal; granting artificial early progress moves the person into the accelerating region and reframes "start" as "continue."
- **Apply.** Never render 0%. Set up onboarding so step one is already complete and *say why it counts* ("you've already told us the room size — three questions left"). Credit trivial completed actions honestly. Show remaining steps, not total steps. **The credited step must be one the user actually took** — reframing a real action as progress is presentation; awarding progress for nothing is the flattery in the next line, and a bar that advances while nothing happens is fabricated computation (§4).
- **Breaks when.** The granted progress is arbitrary and obvious ("you're 40% done!" before anything happened) — the reader spots the flattery and discounts the rest of the page.
- **Dial.** Both poles.

#### The labelling / self-perception effect
- **What.** Telling someone they *are* a certain kind of person nudges behaviour consistent with the label.
- **Mechanism.** Same engine as consistency, applied from the outside: an accepted label becomes self-description, and behaviour follows self-description.
- **Apply.** Label from the reader's own evidence, never from flattery: "if you've already mapped your own workflow, you're the kind of buyer this is built for." Segmenting copy ("for people who read the docs before the pricing") does labelling and self-selection at once.
- **Breaks when.** The label is unearned praise ("smart shoppers like you"), which reads as manipulation and triggers reactance instead.
- **Dial.** Trust pole: evidence-based labels. Urgency pole: identity/tribal labels.

#### The Zeigarnik effect (open loops)
- **What.** Unfinished business stays active in memory and pulls for closure.
- **Mechanism.** Incomplete goals stay accessible until resolved, which is felt as mild curiosity or itch.
- **Apply.** Open exactly one real loop per page and **pay it off**: a diagnostic that names a pattern and offers the fix on click; a section header that poses the reader's own question; an interactive element where each item is a closable question. Resolve every loop before the final CTA.
- **Breaks when.** The loop is clickbait with no payoff, or you open five. Unpaid loops read as wasted time and poison the next section.
- **Dial.** Both. Trust pole: quiet, substantive loops (a real diagnostic). Urgency pole: teaser loops.

### Family C — Framing value

#### Anchoring
- **What.** The first number seen sets the scale everything after it is judged against.
- **Mechanism.** Judgement is comparative; an available reference point is adjusted from, and the adjustment is nearly always insufficient.
- **Apply.** Show the highest legitimate number first — the full-service tier, the cost of the problem, the competitor category price. In a range-based UI (sliders, custom quotes, tip fields), the upper bound sets the perceived norm; choose it deliberately.
- **Breaks when.** The anchor is implausible (a fake "was" price) or the buyer is judging your whole catalogue at once rather than comparing within it. A single-product SaaS is usually judged as a *summary*, where adding a very expensive tier can raise the perceived average and make everything feel expensive — there, add a cheap tier instead. See [07-pricing-psychology.md](07-pricing-psychology.md).
- **Dial.** Both poles. Trust pole: anchor against the true cost of the problem, not a phantom price.

#### Contrast, the decoy, and asymmetric dominance
- **What.** Options are evaluated against their neighbours, not absolutely. Adding a third option that is clearly worse than one specific option makes that option win.
- **Mechanism.** A dominated alternative provides an easy justification ("this one is strictly better than that one"), and people prefer choices they can justify.
- **Apply.** Build three tiers where the intended one is *obviously* superior to a near neighbour on a dimension the buyer already cares about. Keep the comparison legible: one table, aligned rows, differences visible without scrolling.
- **Breaks when.** The decoy is insultingly bad (buyers notice the setup), or the differences are on dimensions nobody values, which just adds choice cost.
- **Dial.** Both poles. Honest version: the decoy is a real option someone actually buys.

#### The center-stage effect
- **What.** In a horizontal set, the middle option is looked at more and chosen more.
- **Mechanism.** The eye lands centrally, then sweeps outward and re-crosses the middle. Repeated fixation and preference reinforce each other.
- **Apply.** Put the tier you want chosen in the center of three, and give it one extra unit of visual weight (a border, a slight lift) — not a loud badge. For sequential presentation, first and last positions are the strong ones instead.
- **Breaks when.** You mark the center with hype ("MOST POPULAR!!") that isn't true. Position does the work; the label is optional and must be factual.
- **Dial.** Both poles; trust pole marks the center with restraint only.

#### Loss aversion and framing
- **What.** The same fact framed as a loss weighs more than framed as an equivalent gain.
- **Mechanism.** Losses loom larger than gains of the same size in how outcomes are valued; framing changes which side of the reference point the outcome sits on.
- **Apply.** Match the frame to the buyer's mindset. Buyers **protecting** something (compliance, uptime, reputation, health) respond to prevention language — protect, keep, avoid, before it costs — and to accuracy-oriented comparison tables. Buyers **pursuing** something (growth, skill, status) respond to promotion language — reach, unlock, become. Do not mismatch; a prevention buyer reads aspiration copy as fluff.
- **Breaks when.** Loss framing becomes manufactured fear about a risk that isn't real, or the benefit copy makes the reader picture the bad outcome ("won't leak" makes them picture leaking). Keep the *problem* section honest and precise, keep the *promise* section positively framed.
- **Dial.** Prevention framing is trust-pole-compatible when it names a real risk. Fear escalation is urgency-pole and is usually the wrong tool for a considered purchase.

#### The endowment effect
- **What.** Once something feels like yours, giving it up feels like a loss, and you value it more.
- **Mechanism.** Ownership shifts the reference point; the item moves from "gain if acquired" to "loss if surrendered."
- **Apply.** Manufacture *honest* pre-ownership: free tiers that keep the user's real work, a configurator that produces "your" setup, a saved draft, a named plan. Language matters — "your workspace", "your results". Trials give ownership; make sure what they built survives the trial.
- **Breaks when.** The endowment is used as a hostage — auto-charging trials, or making exported data hard to retrieve. That is where the effect becomes a dark pattern.
- **Dial.** Both poles.

#### The effort heuristic
- **What.** Perceived effort in making something raises its perceived value; small *required* effort from the user can also raise the value of what they get.
- **Mechanism.** Effort is used as a proxy for quality when quality is hard to judge directly. On the user's side, a trivial earned step creates a sense of deservingness.
- **Apply.** Show the work: process notes, the number of iterations, the craft, genuine enjoyment of making it. For promotions, a trivial redemption step (enter a code, answer one question) can beat a price already discounted on the page. Keep the step **trivial** — real effort destroys the benefit, because the perceived return on effort drops.
- **The added-step caveat.** Deliberately inserting friction so the reward *feels* better is the one move in this family that fails the disclosure test if you get it wrong. It passes only when the step is genuinely trivial, the offer is identical whether or not the buyer takes it, and no one who skips it pays more. It fails the moment the step becomes a toll — a code the buyer must hunt for, a question that harvests data, a hoop that quietly gates the real price. Test: *if I told them I added this step because it makes the discount feel earned, would they laugh or feel played?* If you cannot say it out loud, remove the step and just show the price.
- **Breaks when.** Effort theatre (fake loading bars implying computation that isn't happening) is discovered. Fake computation is deception; a genuinely slow honest process shown honestly is not.
- **Dial.** Trust pole: show craft and provenance. Urgency pole: gamified micro-effort.

### Family D — Attention, memory, and load

#### The Von Restorff (isolation) effect
- **What.** The item that differs from its neighbours is noticed and remembered.
- **Mechanism.** Attention is allocated to local contrast, not absolute loudness. Distinctiveness is relative to context, which means a calm context is *cheaper* to stand out in.
- **Apply.** One primary action per viewport, made distinct by being the only solid fill, plus whitespace isolation. Everything else is a ghost or a text link. If two elements compete, neither wins.
- **Breaks when.** Everything is highlighted. Three "special" badges equal none.
- **Dial.** Both poles — but at the trust pole achieve isolation by *demoting neighbours*, at the urgency pole by *amplifying the target*.

#### The mere-exposure effect
- **What.** Repeated exposure to a stimulus increases liking for it, even without any argument.
- **Mechanism.** Repetition builds fluency; the resulting ease is misread as familiarity-driven liking. Same engine as §1.1.
- **Apply.** Consistency is the lever: one logo lockup, one accent, one button style, one voice, repeated everywhere including email, invoices, and error states. Repeat the *same* CTA wording down a long page rather than inventing variants.
- **Breaks when.** The stimulus is already disliked (repetition deepens dislike), or repetition becomes saturation in paid media, where wear-out flips response negative.
- **Dial.** Both poles. Handle in [../build/09-design-system-and-tokens.md](../build/09-design-system-and-tokens.md).

#### The peak-end rule
- **What.** An experience is remembered by its most intense moment and its ending, not its average.
- **Mechanism.** Memory encodes salient snapshots rather than integrating over duration.
- **Apply.** Deliberately design one peak (the moment the tool shows the reader something about themselves; the unboxing; the first successful action) and design the end (post-purchase confirmation, the last section of the page, the offboarding email). Never end on an admin screen.
- **Breaks when.** You engineer a peak that the product cannot repeat — the second visit sets the new baseline and the drop is what gets remembered.
- **Dial.** Both poles.

#### Priming — and its real limits
- **What.** Exposure to a cue makes related concepts temporarily more accessible, biasing the next judgement.
- **Mechanism.** Spreading activation, time-limited.
- **Honest limits.** This is the area of social psychology where large effects from small, incidental cues have most often failed to replicate. Treat priming as a **tie-breaker inside your own page**, never as a strategy. Do not build a business case on it, and do not claim it as an effect size to a client.
- **Apply.** Keep it to the strong, unmysterious version: what sits immediately next to a thing colours that thing. Put the reassurance directly under the button; keep the security section before the price; don't put a stock photo of a stressed person next to your calm promise.
- **Breaks when.** Cited as magic. If your page only works because of a subtle prime, the page doesn't work.
- **Dial.** Both poles, at low volume.

#### Choice overload — and how to chunk
- **What.** Past a small number of options, choice quality and completion rate fall, and regret rises.
- **Mechanism.** Small sets are apprehended at a glance; larger sets must be counted and compared, which raises cognitive cost and the fear of choosing wrongly.
- **Apply.** Rules of thumb: **≤4 items per visible group**; if you have more, **chunk into ≤4 labelled groups of ≤4** so each decision stays small (a 12-item menu becomes "pick a group, then pick an item"). Three pricing tiers, three feature pillars, ≤4 nav sections. Provide a default and a one-line "which is right for me". Prefer 3 spacious options over 4 cramped ones.
- **What the cap governs.** Options the reader must *choose between*. A set they merely scan — a capability grid, an included-features strip — is not a choice and does not pay the comparison cost; it may run to six ([../build/08-page-architecture-and-section-recipes.md](../build/08-page-architecture-and-section-recipes.md) §3.6). The tell is whether any item carries its own action or price. If it does, it is a choice, and the cap is four.
- **Breaks when.** The reader arrives with a specific target in mind (searching for a known SKU) or the browsing itself is pleasurable — in both, more options are fine or better.
- **Dial.** Both poles. Layout mechanics in [05-visual-attention-and-layout.md](05-visual-attention-and-layout.md).

### Family E — Urgency and autonomy

#### Scarcity — honest forms only
- **What.** Limited availability raises perceived value and accelerates decisions.
- **Mechanism.** Restriction signals demand, threatens future freedom of choice, and makes the loss of the opportunity concrete.
- **Three kinds, three uses.** *Limited supply* (a genuinely capped edition or roster) works for status and identity goods. *Limited quantity* ("two consultation slots left this week", true) works as demand evidence for rational purchases. *Limited time* (a real deadline: a cohort start, a price change you will actually make) works to trigger the start of a considered decision.
- **Apply.** Only state a constraint that exists in your systems and would be discoverable by an auditor. If a real constraint exists, state the *reason* for it ("we take eight clients a quarter because each gets a weekly call"). Prefer status framing ("founding rate, locked while it lasts") over depletion framing at the trust pole.
- **Breaks when.** It is fabricated, or repeated so often that the deadline is visibly evergreen. Discovered fake scarcity destroys every other trust element on the page at once.
- **Dial.** Urgency pole by default. At the trust pole, use only cohort/roster/price-change scarcity, stated once, without a ticking clock.

#### Psychological reactance — and the freedom-to-choose antidote
- **What.** When people feel their freedom is being restricted, they push back — sometimes doing the opposite of what is asked.
- **Mechanism.** Freedom of action is defended like a possession; a heavy-handed ask is experienced as a threat to it, and the threatened option becomes more attractive.
- **Apply.** Explicitly restore autonomy at every ask. Phrases in the family of "it's up to you", "cancel anytime, no call required", "read this later if you'd rather" reliably raise compliance — this is one of the better-replicated compliance findings and it is fully compatible with a calm brand. Also: prefer indirect claims that let the reader draw the conclusion, and add a two-sided "this isn't for you if…" block *before* the price.
- **Breaks when.** The stated freedom is false (cancellation actually requires a phone call). Then you have combined a promise with a dark pattern, which is worse than never claiming it.
- **Dial.** Both poles; indispensable at the trust pole. The high-pressure inverse ("last chance — don't miss out") belongs only where the offer is genuinely time-bound and the stakes are low.

## 3. How to pick which lever

Diagnose first. Most pages fail on one of four things, and each has its own lever family. Guessing
costs you a redesign; the diagnosis is usually visible in analytics plus five user sessions.

| Diagnosis | What you see | Root cause | Lever family | First three moves |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **They don't understand** | high bounce within seconds; visitors can't repeat what you do; support asks "so what is this?" | fluency + category confusion | Engine 1 + concreteness | 1) headline states what it is and for whom  2) match every page to its own name  3) replace abstractions with specifics |
| **They don't believe** | long dwell, deep scroll, no click; FAQ traffic; "is this legit" searches | missing mechanism and proof | reason-why, authority/provenance, social proof, two-sided honesty | 1) mechanism under every promise  2) one specific, named piece of proof  3) own one real drawback before the price |
| **They don't care** | shallow scroll, no section engagement, low return | weak simulation, wrong frame | mental simulation, loss/gain framing, self-reference, Zeigarnik | 1) name their exact situation in their words  2) make the outcome a scene  3) open one real loop and pay it off |
| **They don't act now** | repeat visits, saved carts, "later" replies | effort term too high, or no reason for today | effort reduction, endowed progress, honest scarcity, reactance antidote | 1) name the first step and shrink it  2) grant progress / start above zero  3) if and only if a real deadline exists, state it once — with autonomy language |

**Secondary diagnoses and their levers:**

| Symptom | Lever |
|---|---|
| They understand, believe, and want it — but pick the wrong plan | anchoring, contrast/decoy, center-stage, ≤4 chunking |
| They convert once and never return | peak-end, endowment, endowed progress on the *next* goal |
| They compare you to the wrong category | positioning + anchoring against the true alternative ([../brand/01-positioning-and-category.md](../brand/01-positioning-and-category.md)) |
| Traffic is fine, brand recall is nil | mere exposure via ruthless consistency; one distinctive asset |
| High intent, abandoned checkout | effort term: fields, surprise costs, forced accounts; plus reactance antidote at the final ask |

Order of operations when several apply: **understand → believe → care → act now.** Fixing urgency on
a page nobody understands produces a page nobody understands, faster.

## 4. Ethics: where structuring ends and deception begins

The engines are neutral. Fluency makes a false claim land as easily as a true one. So the honesty
question is never "did I use persuasion" — it is "**is the underlying fact true, and would the
visitor still feel fairly treated if they saw exactly how this element was built?**"

Two tests, both must pass:

1. **The fact test.** Strip the technique. Is the remaining claim literally true, currently true, and checkable by a third party? If the technique is the only thing making it feel true, delete it.
2. **The disclosure test.** If you explained the design choice to the visitor ("we put the mid tier in the middle because people pick the middle"), would they shrug, or feel cheated? A shrug means structuring. A feeling of being tricked means deception.

| Principle | Honest structuring (do this) | Becomes deception when |
|---|---|---|
| Social proof | real, named, specific, consented | invented, bought, or aggregated so it can't be checked |
| Authority | your actual record and provenance | implied affiliations, logo walls of non-customers, borrowed credentials |
| Scarcity | a constraint that exists in your systems | evergreen countdowns, "only 3 left" generated by a script |
| Loss aversion | naming a risk the buyer really faces | manufacturing a fear to sell the cure |
| Anchoring | comparing to the real alternative's real cost | a "was" price that was never charged |
| Decoy | a real option someone genuinely buys | an option built only to be rejected and hidden after purchase |
| Endowment | a trial where their work is theirs and portable | auto-charge after trial, hostage data, hard export |
| Commitment | a ladder where every rung is useful and reversible | a hidden funnel where step one misrepresents step two |
| Reciprocity | a gift that stands alone | a "gift" that immediately extracts contact and resells it |
| Effort heuristic | showing real craft and process | fake computation, invented "personalisation" |
| Labelling | a label the reader's own behaviour earned | flattery engineered to bind them |
| Urgency copy | a deadline you will actually honour | a deadline you will quietly extend, repeatedly |

### Forbidden in this pack — no exceptions, no A/B test

- [ ] Countdown timers on evergreen offers, or any timer that resets on reload.
- [ ] Fabricated scarcity: invented stock counts, fake "X people viewing", phantom seat limits.
- [ ] Invented social proof: written testimonials, fake names/photos, purchased ratings, review gating that suppresses honest negatives.
- [ ] Invented statistics or fabricated study citations — including in your own marketing copy about your own results.
- [ ] Dark-pattern opt-outs: pre-ticked consent, confirmshaming ("No thanks, I like wasting money"), hidden unsubscribe, cancellation that requires a phone call while the page says "cancel anytime".
- [ ] Hidden costs revealed only at the final checkout step.
- [ ] "Was" prices that were never charged, and discounts off an inflated reference.
- [ ] Deceptive urgency language in transactional email subject lines.

If a stakeholder asks for one of these, the substitute is always available: the same lever has an
honest form in the table above. Use that instead, and say why.

Two extra guardrails worth adopting: **run the disclosure test on every persuasive element in
review**, and **never let a persuasion element outlive the fact behind it** — if the founding rate
ends, remove the line the same day.

---

## Apply it

- [ ] The arousal pole is written down in the brand brief, and every tactic on the page is checked against it (invert anything from the wrong pole).
- [ ] Every promise on the page has a mechanism attached with "because" or an equivalent.
- [ ] Every abstraction in the hero and pricing sections has been replaced with a concrete, simulate-able specific.
- [ ] Exactly one primary action per viewport; neighbours demoted rather than the CTA amplified.
- [ ] One real open loop exists and is paid off before the final call to action.
- [ ] Social proof (if present) is named, situational, and specific — or honestly absent, with mechanism and provenance carrying the trust load.
- [ ] One genuine drawback is owned before the price ("not for you if…").
- [ ] An autonomy line sits at every ask, and it is literally true (cancellation really is that easy).
- [ ] Choices are chunked: ≤4 per visible group, three pricing tiers, intended tier center-staged.
- [ ] Onboarding starts above zero and names the first step in effort terms.
- [ ] The effort term has been attacked directly: fields removed, defaults pre-filled, costs shown early.
- [ ] Scarcity, if used, maps to a constraint that exists in the system and would survive an audit.
- [ ] Every element on the page passes both the fact test and the disclosure test.
- [ ] Nothing on the forbidden list appears anywhere, including in email and ads.
