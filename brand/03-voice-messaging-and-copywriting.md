# Voice, Message Architecture and Copywriting

How to define a voice precise enough to hand to a stranger or a model, build a message hierarchy every page
inherits, and write lines that earn belief instead of asking for it. Read this after positioning is settled
and before anyone writes a headline, a button label, or a page brief.

## 1. Set the dial before you set the words

Almost every copy tactic below is direction-dependent. Decide once, at brand level, where you sit:

| | **Calm / premium / trust-first** | **Energetic / urgent / playful** |
|---|---|---|
| Typical brands | clinic, financial tool, B2B platform, consultancy, considered purchase | flash sale, game, event, impulse DTC, youth brand |
| Sentence energy | measured, declarative, few exclamations (ideally none) | punchy, exclamatory, rhythmic repetition |
| Verbs | precise, literal ("you'll see", "we send", "it stops") | vivid, kinetic ("grab", "snap", "race") |
| Scarcity / countdown | absent unless literally true, stated flatly | allowed when true, and made visible |
| Superlatives | replaced with specifics | tolerated as tone, still must be true |
| Emoji | none, or one per long page | fine in moderation, at end of sentences |
| Reader's dominant question | "can I trust this?" | "is this fun / is it worth it right now?" |

Write it into the brand brief as one sentence: *"We are a calm, trust-first brand; we buy belief with
specificity, not volume."* Every rule below that says "turn this up" has an inverse, named where it matters.
**Neutral in both directions:** concreteness, second person, mechanism-backed claims, honest proof, plain
sentence construction — everything else is a dial.

## 2. Define a voice you can hand to someone else

Three adjectives ("friendly, expert, human") is a mood board, not a voice. A usable definition has four
parts: traits with contrast pairs, a banned list, a sample in and out of voice, and a tie-breaker rule.

### 2.1 Traits (pick 3–5, each with a "we do / we never" pair)

The contrast pair is what makes a trait operational. Worked example — an invented B2B invoicing tool, calm
end of the dial:

| Trait | We do | We never |
|---|---|---|
| **Plain** | Name the object: "the invoice", "your client's email", "the reminder". | Use abstractions when a noun exists: "solutions", "workflows", "assets". |
| **Specific** | Give the number, the step, or the duration: "five fields", "sends Monday 09:00". | Say "fast", "easy", "powerful" without the thing that makes it so. |
| **Even-toned** | State the outcome once, calmly, and stop. | Stack exclamation marks, or promise transformation. |
| **On the reader's side** | Say what the product does not do, and who it is wrong for. | Hide the limit until after the payment page. |
| **Practical-warm** | Use contractions and short asides where a colleague would. | Use jokes that cost the reader a second read. |

Rules for your own set:
- Each **"we never"** must name something a real competitor does. If nobody would do the opposite, the trait
  is filler.
- Include at least one trait that costs you something ("we name the limit"). Voices with no cost read as ads.
- Three traits suits a small site; five is the ceiling. Past that, writers ignore the list.

### 2.2 The banned-words list

Ban words that carry a positive charge but survive deletion — if the sentence means the same without the
word, it was decorative. Start here, then add your category's own offenders:

`quality` · `powerful` · `robust` · `seamless` · `world-class` · `best-in-class` · `cutting-edge` ·
`next-level` · `revolutionary` · `game-changing` · `disruptive` · `unlock` · `empower` · `elevate` ·
`supercharge` · `leverage` (as a verb) · `solutions` (as a product noun) · `journey` · `effortless` ·
`curated` · `artisanal` · `passionate` · `synergy` · `innovative`

Two extra bans:
- **Pseudo-perfect claims.** "100%" attached to something with no defined perfect state ("100% quality",
  "100% tasty") reads as padding; consumer-psychology work suggests readers notice and discount it. Keep
  "100%" only as a literal measurement (100% recycled paper, 100% of your data exportable).
- **Impressive-but-empty modifiers.** Unfamiliar or vague descriptors tend to raise price expectations while
  lowering quality impressions — the reader senses a word doing work it cannot back.

The rule to hand to a writer: *if you cannot replace the adjective with a fact, delete it.*

### 2.3 The two sample paragraphs

End the voice doc with the same paragraph written twice — the most useful artifact you can give a
freelancer or a model, because it teaches by contrast instead of instruction.

**Out of voice (invoicing SaaS):**

> Our revolutionary invoicing platform empowers modern businesses to unlock effortless cash flow with a
> seamless, best-in-class experience. Say goodbye to admin — and hello to growth!

**In voice:**

> Invoices go out the day you finish the work, then chase themselves until they're paid. Five fields, no
> accountant, no reminder emails you have to write yourself. If your clients pay by bank transfer, this is
> built for you. If you need multi-currency payroll, it isn't — yet.

What changed: nouns became concrete, the mechanism appeared, the reader got named, and a limit was said out
loud. That is the whole voice in one paragraph.

### 2.4 The tie-breaker

One line that settles arguments without you: *"When two options both fit the voice, choose the more specific
one. When specificity and warmth conflict, keep specificity."* Use your own, but have one.

## 3. Message architecture: one promise, three pillars, proof under each

Build this once; every page, ad, email and page brief inherits from it.

```
CORE PROMISE        one sentence — the outcome the reader gets, in their words
  ├─ PILLAR 1       reason it's true  →  proof, proof   (mechanism · demo · artifact ·
  ├─ PILLAR 2       reason it's true  →  proof, proof    credential · honest provenance)
  └─ PILLAR 3       reason it's true  →  proof, proof
```

**Core promise rules**
- One sentence, under ~15 words, no conjunction stacking. If it needs "and", you have two promises.
- Written as an outcome, not a category ("your invoices collect themselves", not "invoicing software").
- Survives the *reversal test*: would a competent rival ever claim the opposite? If nobody would claim
  "invoices you chase manually", your promise is table stakes, not positioning — go back to
  [positioning](01-positioning-and-category.md).

**Pillar rules**
- Exactly three. Two feels thin; four dilutes — consumer-psychology work on feature dilution points the same
  way, extra claims weaken the perceived strength of the ones that matter.
- Pillars are *different kinds of reason*, not three flavours of one: one about the outcome, one about the
  method, one about the risk you remove.
- State each as a short claim a skeptic could argue with.

**Proof rules**
- Every pillar carries at least one **checkable** proof: a mechanism the reader can reason about, a
  screenshot, a document, a named credential, a demo, a real customer who gave permission.
- No proof yet? Say so plainly or drop the pillar. Never invent numbers, ratings, review counts or "trusted
  by thousands". Honest provenance ("built for our own studio first, used daily for a year before anyone
  else saw it") outperforms a fabricated metric and cannot blow up later.

### 3.1 How pages inherit it

| Page | Inherits | Shape |
|---|---|---|
| Home | promise + all three pillars | hero states the promise; one section per pillar, in priority order |
| Product / feature | one pillar, promoted to headline | the other two appear only as short supporting lines |
| Pricing | the risk-removal pillar | promise restated above the tiers; proof becomes the objection block |
| Blog / guide | one proof, expanded | promise appears once, near the end, as the natural next step |
| Email / ad | promise only, or one pillar only | never all three — one idea per unit |
| About | the provenance proof | the origin story is proof, not decoration |

**One page, one job, one pillar in the lead** — a page arguing all three equally is remembered for none.
Section recipes: [page architecture](../build/08-page-architecture-and-section-recipes.md).

## 4. Concreteness is the highest-leverage lever

Change one thing about your copy: turn abstract words into imaginable ones.

**Mechanism.** Concrete language is easier to picture, and the picture does two jobs — the sentence feels
more true (easy processing gets misread as accuracy) and it sticks, because an image leaves a stronger trace
than a category. Nick Kolenda's consumer-psychology synthesis and the older Hopkins-era advertising
tradition agree: specificity out-persuades virtue-signalling.

**The concreteness ladder.** Walk any abstraction down until the reader could draw it:
`it` → `the tool` → `the invoice` → `the overdue invoice sitting in your drafts`

**Where abstractions hide (audit these):** quality, performance, experience, solution, capability, value,
efficiency, innovation, reliability, support, security, flexibility — each a placeholder for a missing fact.

| Abstract | Replace with |
|---|---|
| "easy to use" | the number of steps, fields, or minutes |
| "fast" | the actual duration, or a comparison to a familiar act |
| "reliable" | what happens when it fails, and how often you've seen it |
| "secure" | the specific mechanism and what it excludes |
| "flexible" | two named things it does that rivals don't |
| "great support" | who answers, from where, in what time window |
| "saves you time" | the task that disappears, named |

- **Related words amplify each other.** Same-scene words activate each other: "brew coffee" pulls a stronger
  image than "make coffee". Pick the verb that belongs to the object.
- **Precision signals accuracy.** Round quantities read as estimates, precise ones as measured — "about a
  year" and "52 weeks" carry different confidence. Use precision only where it is true.

## 5. Second person and the reader's simulation

People decide by simulating: they picture the outcome, then subtract the imagined effort. Make the outcome
vivid and the process feel light.

- **Write in second person.** "You" pulls the reader into the scene; "customers/users/businesses" pushes
  them out to watch. Convert every "our customers get…" into "you get…".
- **Present tense for ongoing goods.** "The reminder goes out every Monday" implies a living service; "went
  out" implies something finished. Ongoing framings make a service feel larger and still-happening.
- **Conditional openers trigger simulation.** "If you…" invites the scenario, "when you…" assumes it,
  "after you…" is most vivid because it speaks from the far side — so it belongs to brands that can back it.
- **Make the process concrete too.** "Create a username and password" is easier to picture than "sign up",
  and easier-to-picture actions feel easier to do.
- **One protagonist, one vantage point.** Switching between "users", "you" and "teams" inside a section
  breaks the running mental image and costs comprehension.
- **Don't over-vivid the outcome.** A detailed enough fantasy can partly satisfy the desire it was meant to
  create. Show enough to want it; leave the last step to the product.

## 6. Benefit vs feature vs mechanism — and why "because" wins

Three layers, all needed, in a fixed order:

| Layer | What it is | Example (dental clinic) |
|---|---|---|
| **Benefit** | what changes for the reader | you leave knowing what it will cost |
| **Feature** | what the product has or does | itemised treatment plan, printed before you go |
| **Mechanism** | why the feature produces the benefit | the plan is generated from the scan during your visit, so nothing is estimated later |

**The trust line formula:** `claim + because + mechanism`. A bare claim invites the reader to supply the
counter-argument; a mechanism gives them something to check instead of something to doubt. This matters most
on the highest-risk lines: privacy, safety, money, data, health, cancellation.

- Weak: "Your data is safe with us." Strong: "Your client list never leaves your device, because the import
  runs locally and only invoice totals sync."
- Every promise in a trust block gets its own mechanism; one shared "we take X seriously" covers nothing.
- The mechanism must be literally true and specific enough to be falsifiable. If you can't write one, you
  don't have the claim — remove it.
- **The inverse (both ends of the dial):** don't amplify protective claims where no worry exists. Loud
  reassurance can plant the fear it answers. Keep privacy and guarantee copy findable (footer, FAQ, the
  moment of the ask) rather than featured in the hero — answer objections where they arise, not before.

## 7. Indirect claims: let the reader draw the conclusion

A direct claim ("our chair is comfortable") makes the reader the audience for an assertion, and skeptics
automatically test the opposite. An implied claim makes the reader the *author* of the conclusion — and
people rarely argue with themselves.

| Direct (weak) | Indirect (stronger) |
|---|---|
| "Our support is responsive." | "Emails answered by the two people who wrote the code, usually before lunch." |
| "The course is practical." | "You finish week one with a recorded talk you can watch back." |
| "Our kettle is durable." | "The one on our test bench has boiled twice a day since 2019." |
| "We're secure." | Show the certification, the audit date, the architecture diagram. |

How to apply:
- Replace an adjective with the **scene that produces it**; prefer an artifact (certificate, log,
  screenshot, timestamp) over an assertion.
- Leave the last inferential step to the reader. State the fact; don't append "…which means we're the best."
- **Limit:** never imply what isn't literally true. Implication is a delivery method, not a loophole — an
  implied claim you couldn't defend in writing is a deceptive claim.

## 8. Prevention-focused vs promotion-focused readers

Readers arrive in one of two motivational modes; the same offer must be framed differently for each.

| | **Prevention-focused** | **Promotion-focused** |
|---|---|---|
| Wants | to avoid loss, error, embarrassment, risk | to gain, grow, win, upgrade |
| Typical context | finance, health, legal, security, anything with a downside | growth tools, hobbies, aspiration, entertainment |
| Verbs that fit | protect, keep, avoid, prevent, stay, hold | reach, grow, get, build, win |
| Proof that lands | guarantees, certifications, reversibility, precedent | possibility, upside, ambition, examples of gains |
| Headline shape | "keep X from happening" | "get to X" |

Application rules:
- Match the frame to the **decision**, not your mood: the same person is promotion-focused choosing a course
  and prevention-focused choosing insurance.
- Cheap/impulse decisions tolerate a promotion frame; expensive or irreversible ones default to prevention.
- If a page serves both, split by section rather than blending — one gain-framed, one risk-framed, each
  internally consistent.
- **Abstraction pairs with distance and price.** Far-off or expensive decisions get judged in broad "why"
  terms, near and cheap ones in concrete "how" terms: *why* language high on the page, *how* language closer
  to the click. Number framing: [pricing psychology](../psychology/07-pricing-psychology.md).

## 9. The two-sided argument

Naming one real drawback before the ask tends to raise credibility — a source that volunteers a cost reads
as someone not managing you. It also self-selects: wrong-fit buyers leave before they pay, protecting your
refund rate and your reviews.

- Pick a drawback that is **true, verifiable and irrelevant to your core buyer.** "Not built for teams over
  fifty" costs nothing if you sell to solo operators — and naming an irrelevant limit can even sharpen the
  sense that the product is specialised for the reader's case.
- State it plainly and early — before pricing, never in a footnote after it.
- Don't resolve it with a wink. "Our only flaw is that we care too much" destroys the effect and the trust.
- Canonical format: a **For you / Not for you** block, two columns, three to five lines each.
- **Skip it** when the drawback is a deal-breaker for your main segment (fix the product instead), or in
  formats too short to resolve it.

## 10. Order effects: primacy and recency

Readers remember the first item and the last item of any list or sequence, and skim the middle.

| Structure | Put here |
|---|---|
| Bullet list (3–5 items) | **First:** the sharpest differentiator. **Last:** the second sharpest. Middle: the ones that merely have to be present. |
| Page sections | **First:** what it is + why care. **Last:** the ask, restated with the same words as the promise. |
| Pricing feature list | First line of the intended tier = the thing no rival has. |
| FAQ | First question = the objection that actually blocks the sale, not the easiest one. |
| Email | Subject and first line carry the whole message; the last line carries the action. |

- **Cap persuasive lists at 3–5 items.** Beyond that, everything is middle. A *reference* list the reader
  only scans (an included-features strip, a spec table) can run longer — up to about seven before it stops
  being a list ([../psychology/06-color-and-typography.md](../psychology/06-color-and-typography.md) §8) —
  but nothing the reader has to weigh belongs in it.
- **Don't strand an argument in whitespace.** A lone claim under a large empty block reads as thin — the
  reader infers you had nothing else to add. Give it its supporting line, or tighten the block ([visual
  attention and layout](../psychology/05-visual-attention-and-layout.md)).

## 11. Open loops, peak and ending

An unresolved question holds attention, and people judge an experience mostly by its most intense moment and
its last one. Design all three deliberately.

- **Open a loop early:** a question the page will answer, a named thing not yet shown, an interactive
  element returning a partial result.
- **Close it** at the final CTA or inside the product — anything opened must be paid off, or the reader
  leaves feeling tricked. **The line:** a loop promising information you never deliver is clickbait.
- **Calm version:** a genuine question ("what would this look like with your numbers?") answered by a demo.
  **High-arousal version:** teaser headlines and countdowns.
- **Completion cues** lighten the rest: a first step shown as already done ("plan picked — two fields left")
  makes a sequence feel easier to continue. Only claim a step is complete if it genuinely is.
- **The peak** is the single best moment on the page: the demo that reflects the reader's own input, the
  line that names their situation exactly, the proof nobody else has. If you can't name it, the page has none.
- **The ending** restates the core promise in the *same words* as the hero (repeated phrasing builds
  familiarity and belief; new phrasing reads as a new, unproven claim), carries one CTA, and closes on the
  reader's freedom rather than your pressure. Never end on legal text or a link farm — keep the footer quiet.

## 12. Headlines

**Patterns that hold up**

| Pattern | Shape | Generic example |
|---|---|---|
| Outcome-first | `<result> without <the usual cost>` | "Paid on time without chasing anyone" |
| Transformation | `<old painful state> → <new calm state>` | "From 40 unpaid invoices to a Monday you don't dread" |
| Self-reference question | names the reader's exact situation | "Still writing reminder emails by hand?" |
| What-it-is + who-for | `<category> for <role>, so <benefit>` | "Invoicing for solo consultants, so nothing goes uncollected" |
| Named mechanism | `<benefit>, because <mechanism>` | "Every invoice chases itself, because reminders are scheduled at send" |
| Concrete number | one real, checkable figure | "Five fields. One invoice. Zero follow-ups." |

**Patterns that only look like they work**

| Anti-pattern | Why it fails |
|---|---|
| Poetic abstraction ("Reimagine the way you work") | reader cannot tell what the product is; fails the five-second test |
| Superlative claim ("The best X on the market") | unverifiable, so it is discounted and discounts the lines near it |
| Question with an obvious "no" ("Want to lose money?") | insults the reader and triggers resistance |
| Stacked promise ("Faster, cheaper, easier, safer") | four claims dilute each other and none carry proof |
| Fake urgency ("Only today!") when untrue | the single fastest way to lose a trust-first brand's credibility |

**Rules**
- Hero headline answers *what is this*; subhead answers *why care*; button answers *what do I do*. Three
  questions, three elements, no overlap.
- Prefer roles to actions where honest: "for freelancers" implies a permanent identity and a peer group,
  "for freelancing" only an activity. Flip side — an identity label can intimidate beginners ("for leaders"
  reads as out of reach), so pair it with a growth framing when your reader is new.
- Digits beat spelled-out numbers for scanning. Emphasise a key number with size or weight, not colour: on a
  calm brand saturation belongs to the one primary action.

## 13. Microcopy around the ask

The smallest text does the heaviest lifting, because it is read at the moment of decision.

| Slot | Job | Calm-brand example | High-arousal inverse |
|---|---|---|---|
| Button label | name the destination or outcome, in words the reader would say | "See your first invoice" | "Start now" |
| Proximity assurance (directly under) | pre-answer the three costs: money, time, commitment | "No card. Takes two minutes. Cancel any time." | "Instant access · secure checkout" |
| Freedom-to-choose line | defuse resistance by restating their control | "Leave whenever you like — export everything on the way out." | "No pressure — browse first" |
| Form helper text | shrink perceived effort | "Two fields. We'll fill the rest from your domain." | same |
| Error text | fix, never scold | "That email is missing an @ — add it and we'll continue." | same |
| Post-click confirmation | close the loop, set the next expectation | "Sent. You'll get the first reminder schedule by email in a minute." | same |

Principles behind the slots:
- **Proximity fuses meaning.** Words beside an action get absorbed into the image of taking it — put the
  reassurance next to the button, not in a distant footer paragraph.
- **Positive framings of negatives.** "Leak-proof" beats "doesn't leak"; the negated version makes the reader
  picture the leak first. Same for "cancel any time" over "no lock-in traps".
- **Reactance antidote.** Any strong ask creates a pull against it; one short sentence restoring the reader's
  freedom softens it and costs nothing.
- **Assurance beats pressure long-run.** Pressure cues (stock counters, timers, "X people viewing") tend to
  lift the immediate click. The cost lands later and off the dashboard that measured the lift: a decision
  made under manufactured time pressure is a decision the buyer had less reason to endorse afterwards, which
  shows up as refunds, chargebacks, churn and complaints rather than as a worse conversion rate. Treat that
  as a mechanism to reason from and a risk to price in, not as a measured effect size — and if you want the
  number for *your* business, the guardrail metric in
  [measurement](../ops/14-measurement-and-experimentation.md) §9 is where it comes from. On a trust-first
  brand the question does not arise: pressure cues are off.
- **Button text must sound natural said aloud**, in first person — cutesy labels and exclamation marks create
  a small wrongness the reader attributes to the action itself.

## 14. Readability targets

Craft defaults, not findings — but the defaults that survive contact with real pages.

| Target | Value | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Reading level | grade 7–9 for marketing pages; grade 9–11 for technical docs | easy processing is read as more credible, regardless of the reader's education |
| Average sentence | 14–18 words | long enough to carry an idea, short enough to hold |
| Longest sentence in a block | ≤ 30 words | past that, the reader re-reads |
| Paragraph on a landing page | 1–3 sentences | scanning behaviour, not laziness |
| Words per hero headline | ≤ 12 | it must land in a glance |
| Sentence rhythm | vary lengths deliberately; never four short sentences in a row | uniform rhythm becomes monotonous and readers disengage |
| Read-aloud test | every hero, CTA and pricing line | readers subvocalise; awkward-to-say is awkward-to-read |

Also: keep one idea per sentence, and open a sentence with the object that closed the last one when a
passage needs to flow. Prefer the shorter synonym unless precision suffers. Heavy alliteration is worth
avoiding in body copy — it pulls attention to the sound of the sentence and away from its content, which is
the opposite of what body copy is for — but treat that as a style preference rather than a finding, and keep
it for headlines and names where the stickiness is the point
([02 §6](02-identity-archetype-and-naming.md)).

## 16. Anti-patterns

| Anti-pattern | What it looks like | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Hype | "revolutionary", "game-changing", "insane results" | one concrete fact instead |
| Superlative without proof | "the best", "the leading", "#1" | a named comparison you can defend, or nothing |
| Exclamation stacking | more than one "!" on a page | delete all; if the sentence needs one, rewrite the sentence |
| Fake urgency | timers that reset, "only 2 left" on a digital product | remove; use an honest cadence ("prices change in January") |
| Invented proof | fabricated ratings, made-up counts, imagined testimonials | ship with honest provenance until real proof exists — this is a hard rail |
| Jargon as proof | acronym walls in place of an explanation | replace with the mechanism in plain words |
| Cutesy filler | "Oopsie!", "Let's get you sorted, superstar!" | write what a competent colleague would say |
| We-centric copy | "Our mission is to empower…" | rewrite from "you" |
| Voice drift | the blog sounds like a different company than the pricing page | run the voice QA below on every new surface |
| Dark-pattern microcopy | shaming decline links ("No thanks, I like overpaying") | keep both options neutral and polite; a passive-aggressive decline damages the brand and provokes resistance |

**Where the line is.** Persuasion becomes manipulation when it depends on the reader being wrong about a
fact. Framing a true benefit vividly is legitimate; implying a certification you don't hold, inventing
scarcity, quoting a review you wrote or hiding a limit until after payment are not framings — they are false
statements with a design budget. Test: *would this line survive the reader learning exactly how it was
built?* If yes, ship it. If no, delete it.

## 17. Voice QA (run before publishing any new surface)

1. Read it aloud. Anything you stumble over gets rewritten.
2. Search the banned list (zero hits) and count exclamation marks (zero on a calm brand).
3. Highlight every adjective. Each must be backed by a fact within two lines, or be deleted.
4. Find the mechanism on every trust claim. Missing mechanism = missing claim.
5. Check pronouns: "you" should outnumber "we" comfortably.
6. Confirm one page, one job, one lead pillar.
7. Name the peak and the ending out loud — if you can't, the page has neither.
8. Verify every number, name, quote and credential against a source you can open.

Handing this to a model: paste the traits table, the banned list and *both* sample paragraphs into the system
prompt — models copy contrast pairs far better than adjectives. Templates: [prompt pack](../ops/16-prompt-pack.md).

## Apply it

- [ ] Write the dial sentence (calm/premium vs energetic/urgent) into the brand brief.
- [ ] Define 3–5 voice traits, each with a "we do / we never" pair a real competitor violates.
- [ ] Publish the banned-words list, including pseudo-perfect "100%" claims and empty modifiers.
- [ ] Write the same paragraph in voice and out of voice; store both in the brand brief.
- [ ] Draft the core promise in one sentence under ~15 words and run the reversal test on it.
- [ ] Fix exactly three pillars, each a different kind of reason, each with one checkable proof.
- [ ] Map every planned page to one lead pillar and one job.
- [ ] Run a concreteness pass: replace each abstraction from the audit list with a fact, number or scene.
- [ ] Add "because + mechanism" to every trust, privacy, safety and money claim.
- [ ] Choose the reader's frame (prevention vs promotion) per page and write the "For you / Not for you"
  block before pricing.
- [ ] Reorder every bullet list so the sharpest item is first and the second sharpest last, and name the
  page's peak moment plus an ending that restates the promise in the hero's exact words.
- [ ] Write the button label, proximity assurance and freedom-to-choose line for every primary CTA.
- [ ] Run the voice QA checklist and verify every number and credential against a real source.
