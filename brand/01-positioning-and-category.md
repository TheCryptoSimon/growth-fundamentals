# Positioning and Category Strategy

The first decision in the pack, and the one every later decision inherits. Positioning fixes *who* the brand
is for, *what* it is compared against, and *the one thing* it claims. Read this before naming anything,
writing a word of copy, or choosing a colour — those are downstream.
If you only do one thing here: fill the statement in [§7](#7-the-positioning-statement) and survive the tests in [§13](#13-pressure-testing-a-position).

## 1. What positioning actually is

Positioning is **the slot you occupy in a specific person's head, relative to the alternatives they already
know about**. It is a fact about the customer's memory, not a sentence on your homepage. You have a position
when a defined group of people, unprompted, can say what you are for and what you beat the obvious
alternative at.

| Belief | Reality |
|---|---|
| "Positioning is our tagline." | A tagline *expresses* a position. Great position, bad tagline is common — and fixable. The reverse isn't. |
| "Positioning is what we're good at." | It is what you're good at **that a specific buyer values more than the alternative gives them**. Unvalued excellence is a hobby. |
| "Position broadly so we don't exclude anyone." | Memory works by contrast. A position that excludes nobody is stored nowhere. |

**Mechanism.** Memory is associative and comparative: a new thing is filed next to things that already exist
and remembered only for how it *differs*. If the differences are small, the brain files you as "another one
of those" and defaults to the cheapest or most familiar option. Positioning is the deliberate choice of what
you're filed next to, and of the one difference you want stored.

**Operational definition.** You are positioned when you can answer these four in one breath, and a stranger
who heard it can repeat it back a day later: (1) who is it for, narrow enough to name; (2) what do they use
instead today; (3) what one thing is better; (4) why should anyone believe that — mechanism, not adjective.

## 2. Name the dial before you choose a position

Positioning tactics split hard across the arousal dial. Decide the register **first**, because half the
advice below inverts.

| | **Calm / premium / trust end** | **Energetic / urgent / challenger end** |
|---|---|---|
| Typical brands | Advisory, clinical, financial, B2B tools, high-consideration services | DTC impulse goods, consumer apps, events, promotions, youth brands |
| Positioning move | **Specialist authority**: narrow scope, deep proof, understatement | **Named enemy**: pick a villain (the incumbent, the old way) and campaign against it |
| Category move | Sub-categorise quietly; let the buyer discover the distinction | Declare a new category loudly and repeat the name relentlessly |
| Proof style | Mechanism, method, credentials, restraint ("here is exactly how it works") | Demonstration, before/after, spectacle, speed |
| Comparison | Implicit; comparison tables read as insecure at this end | Explicit; head-to-head tables are a core asset at this end |
| Language | Precise, plain, low-adjective | Vivid, punchy, high-contrast |
| Risk if you use the wrong end | Sounds boring, gets ignored | Sounds like hype, destroys trust in a high-stakes purchase |

**Rule:** the higher the perceived risk of the purchase (money, health, career, data, irreversibility), the
further toward the calm end your positioning language must sit — *even if the differentiator itself is
dramatic*. A dramatic claim delivered calmly reads as confidence; a modest claim delivered loudly reads as
desperation. Register is set here, enforced in [03-voice-messaging-and-copywriting.md](03-voice-messaging-and-copywriting.md).

## 3. Segmentation and the beachhead

Segmentation splits a market into groups that **behave differently in a way you can act on**. If two
"segments" buy for the same reason, on the same trigger, against the same alternatives, they are one segment.

**Useful axes (pick 2, not 6):** *trigger* (the event that puts them in market this week) · *current
solution* (what they'd use if you didn't exist, including "nothing" and "a spreadsheet") · *constraint* (the
resource they're short of) · *consequence of failure* (how bad it is if the job goes wrong).

Useless on their own: age bracket, industry vertical alone, company size alone, "people who care about
quality". Demographics describe who they are; they rarely predict what they buy.

**The beachhead.** Your first position targets one segment small enough that you can plausibly become the
obvious choice in it within a year. Sizing test — all four must be true:

- [ ] You can name at least 20 real prospective buyers in it without research.
- [ ] They talk to each other (a forum, an association, a conference, a subreddit, a group chat).
- [ ] They share one dominant trigger and one dominant alternative.
- [ ] Winning it gives you a credible right to attack an adjacent segment next.

**Mechanism:** word of mouth and reference-selling compound only inside a connected group. Spread across five
disconnected segments and every sale starts from zero. The beachhead is a compounding decision, not a modesty
exercise — and nobody remembers that a large brand started narrow.

## 4. The jobs-to-be-done lens

People do not buy products, they hire something to make progress in a situation. A "job" is the outcome plus
the circumstance, never the product. "A faster kettle" is a solution; "when guests arrive unannounced, get
hot drinks in hand before the conversation goes flat" is a job. Format:
`When [situation], I want to [motivation], so I can [expected outcome].`

**How to interview for it.** Ask about the *last time*, not about preferences. Preferences are invented on
the spot; behaviour is remembered.

| Ask | Not |
|---|---|
| "Walk me through the last time this went wrong." | "What features would you like?" |
| "What did you try before that?" | "Would you use a tool that…?" |
| "When did you first realise you needed something?" | "How important is X to you?" |
| "Who else was involved in the decision?" | "Would you pay for this?" |
| "What nearly stopped you buying?" | "Do you like it?" |

Practical shape of the research (the voice-of-the-customer tradition, associated with Griffin and Hauser's
work on customer-need elicitation):

1. **Collect.** In-depth interviews are the richest source; reviews of competing products, support tickets and
   forum threads are a cheap supplement. The yield curve flattens fast — a couple of dozen in-depth
   interviews typically surfaces most of the *distinct* needs in a market; further ones confirm rather than
   discover.
2. **Code.** Every time a need is expressed, restate it as one atomic sentence and log it as a row. One quote
   often yields three needs.
3. **Consolidate.** Dedupe, merge near-identicals, group into themes.
4. **Quantify.** Survey the segment and rate each need twice: **importance** and **how well existing options
   satisfy it**.
5. **Read the quadrants.**

| | Low satisfaction today | High satisfaction today |
|---|---|---|
| **High importance** | **Position here.** Important and badly served = your opening. | Table stakes. Must have it; it wins nothing. |
| **Low importance** | Experimental. Test cheaply, don't build a brand on it. | Over-served. The market has over-invested; you can cut. |

**Do not skip step 4.** Founders systematically over-rate needs they personally feel. The importance ×
satisfaction gap is what turns an opinion into a position.

## 5. Compete, sub-categorise, or create

Three ways to relate to an existing category. They are not equally priced.

| Strategy | What you're saying | Cost | When it's right | Main risk |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Compete in the category** | "We are a [known category], and we're the best at [attribute]." | Lowest. Demand already exists; buyers already search the term. | The category is growing, buyers are unsatisfied, and you have a genuine attribute edge. | You inherit the category's price ceiling and get compared feature-for-feature. |
| **Sub-categorise** | "We are the [known category] **for** [segment] / **that** [distinct mechanism]." | Medium. You ride existing demand while carving out an uncontested corner. | Almost always the best first move. | Getting flattened back into the parent category if the qualifier is weak. |
| **Create a category** | "This is not a [known category] at all. It is a [new thing]." | Highest. You must fund the education of an entire market before anyone shops. | You genuinely break the existing scoring rules and have runway to teach. | Nobody searches your category name; you spend years being explained rather than chosen. |

**Default to sub-categorising.** It is the only one of the three that gets you both existing demand and
defensible difference. "The [category] for [narrow segment]" is the highest-return sentence in early
positioning, and it converts directly into a landing-page H1 and a search strategy.

**If you do create a category**, budget for three things people forget: a plain-language definition you
repeat identically everywhere, an explicit bridge from the old category ("if you're using X today…"), and a
keyword layer targeting the *old* words while the new ones have no volume ([§12](#12-positioning-must-survive-contact-with-search-demand)).

## 6. Frame of reference control

Whatever you compare yourself to sets the scoring rules — and buyers will pick a comparison for you if you
don't pick one first. The frame determines which attributes count and what price feels normal.

Same product, three frames:

| Frame | Buyer's implied question | What now counts | Price anchor |
|---|---|---|---|
| "vs. cheaper software in the same category" | Which is cheapest per seat? | Feature count, price | The cheapest competitor |
| "vs. hiring a part-time person" | Is this cheaper than a salary? | Reliability, coverage, effort saved | A monthly wage |
| "vs. doing nothing" | Is the pain worth solving at all? | Cost of the status quo | Zero — the hardest frame |

**Rules for frame control:**

1. **Choose the frame where your differentiator is decisive**, then state it in the first 10 words of the hero.
2. Pick a frame the buyer already believes is relevant. A frame they find absurd damages credibility more than
   a bad price.
3. **Never frame against a cheaper category** unless you intend to be the cheap option — the comparison caps
   what you can charge even when you win it.
4. The default frame, absent any effort, is *the status quo*. Most lost deals are lost to "nothing", not to a
   competitor. If you have no evidence of what buyers compare you to, assume inertia and argue against it.
5. On the calm/premium end, set the frame with a single sentence and move on. On the energetic end, an
   explicit side-by-side table is a legitimate asset. Do not put an aggressive comparison table on a
   trust-first page — see the dial in [§2](#2-name-the-dial-before-you-choose-a-position).

## 7. The positioning statement

```
For [WHO — a nameable segment]
who [SITUATION / TRIGGER / PAIN — in their words],
[BRAND] is the [CATEGORY — the frame of reference]
that [DIFFERENTIATED BENEFIT — one, comparative],
because [MECHANISM / REASON TO BELIEVE — a fact, not an adjective].
```

Slot rules:

| Slot | Must be | Fails if |
|---|---|---|
| WHO | Nameable; you can list 20 real examples | "SMBs", "busy professionals", "everyone with X" |
| SITUATION | A moment with a trigger, phrased as the buyer says it | An abstraction ("inefficiency", "lack of visibility") |
| CATEGORY | A frame the buyer already holds, or a parent category plus qualifier | A word you invented that nothing anchors to |
| BENEFIT | One, comparative, and outcome-shaped | A list; a feature; "high quality" |
| MECHANISM | A verifiable *how* | "Because we care about our customers" |

The `because` clause is doing more work than it looks. A reason attached to a claim makes the claim easier to
process and easier to trust — the effect that underlies "reason-why" copywriting — and it also protects you,
because a mechanism you can point at is a claim you can defend. If your `because` can be truthfully written
by a competitor, you have not yet found your differentiator; go back to [§8](#8-finding-a-real-differentiator).

## 8. Finding a real differentiator

A usable differentiator passes three gates. Two out of three is a marketing problem waiting to happen.

| Gate | Question | Test |
|---|---|---|
| **Real** | Is it actually true of the product today, not on the roadmap? | Could a customer verify it in one session? |
| **Provable** | Can a stranger check it without trusting you? | Name the artefact: a demo, a public method page, an audited number, a guarantee with teeth, a spec. |
| **Hard to copy** | Would a competitor need to change something structural to match it? | If a competitor can match it by editing their homepage this afternoon, it is not a differentiator. |

**Where durable difference usually comes from** (ranked by how hard it is to copy):

1. **A structural choice with a cost** — a business model, a pricing model, a service commitment, a refusal to
   do something profitable. Competitors can copy it only by giving something up.
2. **An accumulating asset** — proprietary data, a corpus, a community, an integration nobody else has.
3. **A process or method you can name and publish** — a named method is copyable in substance but not in
   attribution; being first to name it is worth a lot.
4. **A narrow specialisation** — being *only* for one segment. Cheap to adopt, genuinely hard for a
   generalist to match without abandoning revenue.
5. **Craft and taste** — real, but slow to prove and easy to claim falsely. Never the *only* differentiator.

Not differentiators, no matter how strongly you feel: quality, great support, ease of use, "our team",
passion, being customer-obsessed, having good design. Every competitor claims all of these; claims everyone
makes carry no information.

## 9. The one-promise discipline

**A second promise halves the first.** Attention is finite and memory stores contrasts, not lists. Two claims
of equal weight do not double your impact — they force the reader to decide which one you mean, and most
resolve that by remembering neither.

Operational rules:

- One promise per brand. One promise per page. One promise per section.
- Supporting benefits are allowed, but they must visibly *serve* the main promise, not compete with it. A
  supporting benefit sits lower on the page, in smaller type, in a group of three or four peers.
- If two candidate promises both feel essential, they are usually one promise and one mechanism. Test:
  can you join them with "because"? If yes, the second is your `because` clause, not a second promise.
- If they genuinely cannot be joined, you have two segments. Pick one for the beachhead and build the second
  a separate page — see [../build/08-page-architecture-and-section-recipes.md](../build/08-page-architecture-and-section-recipes.md).

## 10. Competitive mapping without lying to yourself

Build a 2×2 — but the axes must be **things buyers weigh when choosing**, not things you happen to measure.

**Method:**

1. Pull the top 8–12 decision criteria straight out of your interview transcripts (§4), in the buyers' words.
2. Drop every criterion that everyone in the category already satisfies (table stakes — they don't
   discriminate) and every criterion nobody cares much about.
3. From what's left, pick the two that are **most important** and **most variable across competitors**. Those
   are your axes.
4. Plot every real alternative — including the spreadsheet, the agency, the in-house hire, and doing nothing.
5. Look for an empty quadrant that is empty *for a good reason you can beat*, not empty because nobody wants
   it.

**Honesty rails for the map:**

- Plot competitors as their customers see them, not as you see them. If you can't, ask three of their
  customers.
- If you land alone in a quadrant on your first attempt, you almost certainly chose flattering axes. Redo it
  with the two axes you least want to be scored on and see where you sit.
- An empty quadrant is a hypothesis, not a victory. It becomes a position only when the importance data from
  §4 says buyers actually want that corner.

## 11. Category-entry points: when should the brand come to mind

Positioning answers "what are we"; category-entry points (CEPs) answer "**at what moments should we be
recalled**". A brand recalled only once someone is already shopping the category has ceded the first half of
the funnel. **Method:** list the situations, triggers and questions that occur *before* someone would search
your category term, each written as the buyer would think it.

| CEP moment (how they'd think it) | Where you must exist to be recalled |
|---|---|
| "This keeps going wrong every Monday." | A diagnostic article on the recurring failure |
| "How much is this actually costing us?" | A calculator or benchmark page |
| "Is there a better way to do X?" | A method page, a comparison of approaches |
| "Someone recommended we look at options." | A category-term page that ranks and converts |
| "The person who used to handle this has left." | A "how to take this over" guide |

Aim for 5–8 CEPs. Each one becomes a content brief and a page. This is the join between positioning and the
search work in [../search/11-seo-fundamentals.md](../search/11-seo-fundamentals.md) and
[../search/12-geo-ai-search.md](../search/12-geo-ai-search.md) — the CEP list *is* your topic map.

## 12. Positioning must survive contact with search demand

A position lives in memory; search lives in language. They come apart constantly, and the failure is
predictable: you invent a crisp, differentiated category term, and nobody types it. **Diagnostic** — take
your CATEGORY slot from §7 and check three things:

1. **Does anyone search it?** Check volume for the exact phrase and its close variants.
2. **Does anyone *say* it?** Search the phrase in forums, reviews and support threads. Zero occurrences in
   buyer language is a stronger warning than zero search volume.
3. **What do they type instead?** Collect the 10–30 phrases they actually use for the same job.

**Then choose a path:**

| Situation | Move |
|---|---|
| Your category term has real demand | Own it. Category page as the primary landing page, brand it hard. |
| Your term has no demand, but the parent category does | **Capture-the-keyword layer**: rank on the parent term and the problem phrases, and introduce your distinction *on the page after arrival*. Position in the H1's qualifier, not in a word nobody searches. |
| Neither the term nor the parent has demand (a genuinely new category) | Rank on the **problem and the old solution** ("how to stop X", "alternatives to Y", "why Z keeps happening"). Demand-creation content, not category content. Budget longer. |
| Demand exists but is owned by three entrenched pages | Attack the long-tail CEPs from §11 first; take the head term later with earned authority. |

**Never distort the position to chase a keyword.** The rule is: the *position* is fixed, the *vocabulary* on
any given page adapts to how that page's visitor phrases the problem. A page can lead with the buyer's words
and still deliver the brand's one promise in the first paragraph. Mechanics for this — page types, entity
wiring, internal linking — are in [../search/11-seo-fundamentals.md](../search/11-seo-fundamentals.md) and
[../search/13-schema-and-technical-wiring.md](../search/13-schema-and-technical-wiring.md).

## 13. Pressure-testing a position

Run all six. A position that fails any of them will fail in the market too, more slowly and more expensively.

| Test | How to run it | Fail signal |
|---|---|---|
| **Sharp-elbow test** | Could a competent competitor credibly claim the **opposite** of your differentiator? | If the opposite is absurd ("we're slow, insecure and hard to use"), your claim carries no information. Only claims with a credible opposite say anything. |
| **Trade-off test** | What are you deliberately *worse* at, and who are you deliberately *not* for? | If you can't name both, you have a wish list, not a position. |
| **Repeat-back test** | Explain it once to five people in the segment. A day later, ask them what you do. | They paraphrase into the generic category ("some kind of software for X"). |
| **Price test** | Would this position let you charge above the category median? | See §14. |
| **Substitution test** | Delete your brand name from the homepage and drop in your closest competitor's. Does anything read false? | Nothing breaks → the page is category boilerplate. |
| **Evidence test** | For each claim, name the artefact that proves it. | Any claim with no artefact must be cut or downgraded to a supporting line. |

**The manipulation line.** Positioning shapes which true comparison a buyer makes — that's legitimate.
It crosses into manipulation when it makes a comparison a buyer *couldn't* make if fully informed: implying a
capability you don't have, defining a category so narrowly that "the only X that Y" is technically true but
misleading, or using invented scarcity or invented authority to make the frame stick. The workable line:
**would you be comfortable if the buyer saw the reasoning behind the frame?** If not, change the frame.
Never invent numbers, ratings, testimonials, awards or research to support a position.

## 14. Pricing power is the test of positioning

The market's verdict on your position is what you can charge. If you cannot price above the category median,
buyers have not registered a difference — regardless of what your homepage says. Signals, most reliable first:

- **Discount pressure.** Discount requests in most deals mean you're being scored on the category's default
  axis (price) — your frame of reference lost.
- **The question you get asked first.** "How much is it?" opening the conversation = commodity position. "How
  does the [mechanism] work?" = differentiated position.
- **Win/loss reasons.** Losing on price is a positioning failure; losing on fit is a positioning *success*
  (you excluded correctly).
- **Willingness to wait.** Buyers who wait for onboarding, a slot or a missing feature have registered a
  difference; buyers who take whatever's available today have not.

Positioning sets the ceiling; price *design* works inside it. Never use pricing tactics to rescue a position
that hasn't been earned — see [../psychology/07-pricing-psychology.md](../psychology/07-pricing-psychology.md).

## 15. Worked example, carried through

Invented brand, generic market. **"Chairtime"** — software for independent dental practices.

**Segment and beachhead.** Not "dental practices". Independent, single-site practices with 2–6 chairs, no
dedicated practice manager, where the owner-dentist personally rebuilds the day's schedule. Nameable,
connected (shared associations and supplier reps), one dominant trigger.

**Job statement.** *When a patient cancels at 7am, I want to refill that chair before the slot arrives, so I
don't lose the day's margin and my team doesn't stand around.*

**Needs quadrant.** "Book appointments" and "send reminders" score high importance / high satisfaction —
table stakes every incumbent covers. "Refill a slot that cancels the same morning" scores high importance /
**low** satisfaction. That gap is the opening.

**Category decision.** Sub-categorise, don't invent. Parent category: *practice management software*.
Qualifier: same-day cancellations. Chairtime becomes "the practice scheduling tool built for same-day
cancellations".

**Frame of reference.** Not "vs. cheaper practice-management suites" — that frame scores feature count and
caps price. Frame instead **vs. an empty chair**: the status quo of a lost hour. The scoring axis becomes
recovered revenue, where the differentiator is decisive.

**Differentiator against the three gates.** A waitlist engine that reads the live appointment ledger and
auto-messages ranked, pre-qualified patients within minutes of a cancellation. *Real* — it ships. *Provable*
— the practice reads a recovered-slots figure off its own data and can watch a demo end-to-end. *Hard to
copy* — it needs the ledger integration and the ranking logic, a structural build rather than a homepage edit.

**Positioning statement.**

> For **independent dental practices of two to six chairs without a dedicated practice manager**,
> who **lose hours of chair time to same-day cancellations they can't refill fast enough**,
> **Chairtime** is the **practice scheduling tool built for same-day cancellations**
> that **refills a cancelled slot before it arrives, instead of just recording that it happened**,
> because **it reads the live appointment ledger and auto-messages a ranked, pre-qualified waitlist within
> minutes of the cancellation.**

**One promise.** *Refill the chair.* Reminders, recalls and reporting sit below the fold as a row of three
supporting benefits. The moment "and full practice reporting" is promoted to the hero, the position blurs.

**Sharp-elbow check.** Could a competitor credibly claim the opposite — "we don't chase same-day
cancellations, we focus on long-horizon recall planning"? Yes, and some do. The claim carries information.
Passes. **Trade-off named:** worse for multi-site groups needing central administration; not for practices
already running a waitlist by hand — both stated on the site, before pricing.

**Dial.** High-stakes clinical buyer → calm/premium end: specialist authority, no countdown timers, no
aggressive competitor table, understated language, mechanism-led proof. The energetic version of the *same*
position — a named enemy ("the empty chair is stealing your week"), head-to-head table, urgency — would suit
a DTC brand and would read as unserious here.

**Search reality.** Nobody searches "same-day cancellation software"; that phrase exists only in Chairtime's
head. Buyers type *"dental appointment reminder software"*, *"reduce dental no-shows"*, *"how to fill last
minute cancellations dentist"*. So: capture-the-keyword layer. The parent-category page takes the head term
and introduces the distinction in the first paragraph after arrival; CEP pages take the problem phrases and a
cost-of-empty-chair calculator. Position unchanged, vocabulary matched to each visitor.

**Pricing power check.** Because the frame is recovered chair time rather than seat licences, price is argued
against recovered revenue, not against the cheapest suite. If deals still open with "how much per month?",
the frame isn't landing and the hero needs rewriting — not a discount.

## 16. Anti-patterns

| Anti-pattern | Why it fails | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| **Positioning on adjectives** ("innovative", "seamless", "powerful") | Every competitor claims them; no credible opposite exists | Replace each adjective with the mechanism that would cause it |
| **Positioning on "quality"** | Unverifiable pre-purchase and claimed universally | Name the specific dimension of quality and the artefact that proves it |
| **Positioning on the founder's taste** | Personal preference isn't evidence of demand; it survives until the first cohort of real buyers | Run §4 and let the importance × satisfaction data pick |
| **Positioning on a word nobody searches or says** | Invisible in search and unrepeatable in conversation | Keep the concept, change the surface vocabulary; add the capture-the-keyword layer (§12) |
| **Positioning on a feature** | Features get copied in a quarter; positions built on them expire | Move up to the outcome, keep the feature as the `because` |
| **Two promises** | Each halves the other; readers remember neither | One promise; demote the other to supporting or to its own page |
| **Positioning against a cheaper category** | You win the argument and lose the price | Reframe against the expensive alternative or against the status quo |
| **"For everyone"** | Excludes nobody, so it's stored nowhere | Pick the beachhead; you can widen after you're the obvious choice in it |
| **Category creation by default** | Founders invent a category to avoid comparison, then fund years of education | Sub-categorise instead unless you have runway and a genuine rule-break |
| **Restating the category** ("we are a CRM") | True and useless — no contrast | Add the qualifier that makes it yours |
| **Rewriting the position every quarter** | Positions compound through repetition; resetting resets the memory | Lock it for at least two quarters; change the *expression*, not the position |

## Apply it

- [ ] Named the arousal dial (calm/premium vs energetic/urgent) and written it at the top of the brand brief.
- [ ] Chosen a beachhead segment that passes all four sizing tests in §3.
- [ ] Run at least a dozen last-time-it-happened interviews and coded them into atomic need statements.
- [ ] Scored needs on importance × current satisfaction and identified the high-importance / low-satisfaction quadrant.
- [ ] Written the job statement in `When… I want to… so I can…` form, in the buyer's words.
- [ ] Decided explicitly: compete, sub-categorise, or create — and written down the cost you're accepting.
- [ ] Chosen the frame of reference deliberately, and confirmed it isn't a cheaper category.
- [ ] Filled the §7 statement, every slot passing its rule.
- [ ] Confirmed the differentiator passes real / provable / hard-to-copy, and named the artefact that proves it.
- [ ] Named what you're deliberately worse at and who you're not for — and put it on the site before pricing.
- [ ] Run the sharp-elbow test (a competitor could credibly claim the opposite) and the repeat-back test (five people in the segment, asked a day later).
- [ ] Checked search demand for the category term and built the capture-the-keyword layer if it's thin.
- [ ] Listed 5–8 category-entry points and turned each into a page brief.
- [ ] Verified every claim on the page has an artefact behind it — no invented numbers, ratings or authority.
